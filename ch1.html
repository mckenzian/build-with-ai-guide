Chapter 1 · The architecture

Think Like an Architect

Typing code used to be the hard part of making software. Now AI tools can write a lot of it for you. What they still can't do is decide what should be built, how the pieces fit together, and what "done" looks like. That's your job, and it's the skill this course teaches.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Name the person and their need before you think about features.
  • Tell the real need apart from the first idea for a solution.
  • Break a big idea into small jobs, and write a three-line spec for one of them.
  • Name what can never break.
  • Turn "how you'll know it works" into a test someone else can run.

  2. Start with the person
  3. Break it into small jobs
  4. Write the spec first

A real moment: a vague request, a bad build

From the ALC live-class build

I asked an AI helper for a "Celebrations" board to recognize students. That was the whole request. The AI built it fast: four award cards on the school's front page. But my request never said where the results would come from, or what to show before there were any. So the cards went up empty, and the board was taken down the same day.

  1. Vague request"a Celebrations board"
  2. AI builds fastfour award cards
  3. Cards are emptyno data, no plan for "nothing yet"
  4. Taken downsame day
Key idea

The AI did what I asked. I hadn't asked for enough. A fast builder with no plan just builds a mess faster. The fix isn't a smarter AI. It's a better plan, and that's what this chapter teaches.

Think of it like building a house. An AI tool is a very fast crew of builders. The architect is the person who draws the plan. In this course, that's you. AI researcher Andrej Karpathy describes it the same way: the AI does the generating, and people do the checking (Y Combinator, 2025).

What you need

  • Pen and paper, or any free notes app (Google Docs, for example).
  • A free AI chat assistant. Several popular assistants, such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini and Claude, have offered free versions, but plans change, so check before class. You don't need a paid plan for anything here.
Check the age rules and get school approval first

Some AI assistants are for adults only, and some plans have age limits. Read each tool's age rules, and use only a tool your school has approved (see Chapter 4) and that your family allows.

The build loop

Every project in this course goes round the same five steps. You'll come back to this loop in every chapter.

StepThe question you ask
1 · UnderstandWho is this for, and what do they actually need?
2 · DesignWhat's the smallest useful piece I can build first?
3 · Build and testDoes it do what the plan says?
4 · ObserveWhat really happens when someone uses it?
5 · DecideWhat changes next?

A real example: the live-class tool

I built a live-class tool where students answer questions during class, earn points with their teams, and catch up on anything they missed. I didn't start by writing code. I started with one question: What does a teacher need in the first five minutes of class?

From the ALC live-class build

In my online classes, the first few minutes go to putting students into breakout rooms, and each room is a team in the live-class tool. Matching rooms to teams takes real effort. So when an early version crashed and lost the teams, I had to redo the whole match in the middle of class. That one moment became a rule: team assignments have to survive a crash.

Each lesson in this chapter answers one planning question. Lesson 1.4 is a little different: it doesn't describe one part, it protects all of them.

1.1Start with the person, not the feature

It's tempting to start with cool features: "It should have leaderboards! And dark mode! And sound effects!" Architects start somewhere else: with the person who will use the thing.

Write one sentence about who uses it and what they need. Software teams call this a user story: a short, plain-language note about a person, what they want, and why (Rehkopf, n.d.).

A need"A teacher can tell within seconds whether the class is following."

That sentence names a person (a teacher), a situation (teaching a class) and a need (knowing, fast, if students are keeping up). Every feature you add later should help with that need. If it doesn't, it can wait.

The need is not the first solution

Notice what the sentence doesn't say. It doesn't say "a points system." Points might be one way to meet the need, but so might a quick thumbs-up poll. If you write the solution down as the need, you've made the decision before you understood the problem.

A first idea (a solution)The need underneath
"A points system""A teacher can tell within seconds whether the class is following."
"A homework app with reminders""I keep forgetting which assignments are due tomorrow."

1.2Break it into small jobs

A big idea is hard to build all at once, for you and for an AI tool. So break it into small jobs that can each be built and tested on their own. Experienced teams work this way too: they split work into chunks of a few hours, so problems show up early, while they're still easy to find (Fowler, 2024).

For the live-class tool, the first jobs looked like this:

  1. Joining a class
  2. Showing questions
  3. Collecting answers
  4. Keeping score

Each job is small enough to describe clearly, build on its own, and check before moving on. If "keeping score" has a bug, you fix keeping score. You don't have to untangle the whole app. (In Chapter 2 you'll see a real fix that went wrong and was easy to catch, because it was one small job.)

1.3Write the spec before the build

A spec (short for "specification") is a short note that says three things about one job:

PartExample: a quiz page
What it doesShows one multiple-choice question at a time.
What it doesn't doNo logins and no saving yet.
How you'll know it worksI click an answer and see "Correct" or "Try again."

Compare these two requests:

Vague"Make a quiz app."

Clear"Make a page that shows one multiple-choice question at a time. It does not need logins or saving yet. It works if I can click an answer and see 'Correct' or 'Try again.'"

The second one tells the AI what to build, what to skip, and how to check its work. Karpathy explains why this matters: a vague request means the AI doesn't do quite what you wanted, the check fails, and you end up going round in circles. A little more time spent being concrete gets you moving faster (Y Combinator, 2025). Ethan Mollick, a Wharton professor who studies how people use AI, gives the same advice: be very clear about exactly what you want (Mollick, 2024).

A spec is a living document

A spec isn't carved in stone. When you learn something, you update it. After the crash that lost the teams, the spec for "joining a class" got a new line: team assignments survive a crash. The spec is your best current understanding, written down where everyone (including your AI helpers) can see it.

See how small one job's code can be (optional, you can skip this) 
score = 0
if answer == correct_answer:
    score = score + 1

A tiny piece of a "keeping score" job. You won't write code in this course; the point is that a clear, small spec leads to small pieces that are easy to check.

1.4Decide what can never break

Some things matter more than others. An architect names those things early, so every later decision protects them. This list is different from a spec: it isn't about one job, it covers the whole project.

For the live-class tool, two things could never break:

  • Student data. Information about students has to stay safe and private. Ontario's privacy commissioner invites school boards to sign a charter committing to make student privacy "a top priority" (Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario, n.d.).
  • Working during a live class. If the tool fails while a teacher is mid-lesson, that's a real problem for real people.

Once you've named your never-break list, use it to make decisions. Should you add a flashy new feature the day before a big class? Check the list. If it puts a never-break item at risk, the answer is probably "not yet."

From the ALC live-class build

One evening, several finished changes were ready to go live, but my first class the next morning started at 9:15. Shipping a big batch late at night would put "working during a live class" at risk, with no time to fix anything before students arrived. So the rule became: only changes checked by 8 PM go out that night. Everything else waits for the next update.

1.5Make success testable

"It works well" isn't something anyone can check. "How you'll know it works" has to be a test: something a person can actually try, with a clear pass or fail. That's called an acceptance test.

Acceptance test: a check, agreed before you build, that says "if this happens, the job is done."
  1. Needwho and what
  2. Specdoes, doesn't, how you'll know
  3. Acceptance testtry it: pass or fail
StepExample
NeedA teacher can tell within seconds whether the class is following.
SpecThe teacher's screen shows how many students have answered the current question. It doesn't show who answered what. It works if the count is right and updates quickly.
Acceptance testFive test students answer. Within five seconds, the teacher's screen says "5 of 5 answered."

If a classmate can read your test and run it without asking you anything, it's a good test. You'll use tests like this in every chapter from now on.

What you must check yourself

  • Is the need a need, not a solution? No feature names in your one sentence.
  • Does every job have all three spec lines? Especially "what it doesn't do."
  • Could someone else run your acceptance test? Pass or fail, no guessing.

Try it

Exercise 1: Plan your own app

Pen and paper, or a free notes app · about 30 minutes

  1. Pick an app idea, anything you'd actually like to exist. Write who it's for in one sentence (person + situation + need). Check it's a need, not a solution.
  2. List four small jobs the app needs.
  3. Pick one job and write a three-line spec: what it does, what it doesn't do, how you'll know it works. Turn the last line into an acceptance test.
  4. Write your never-break list: one to three things your app must always get right.
  5. Hand your spec to someone else. Can they tell what you're building, and could they run your test? Fix whatever they had to ask about.

Exercise 2: Vague vs clear

Any free AI chat assistant · about 15 minutes

  1. Open a free AI chat assistant. Ask something vague, like "Make me a study app." Read what you get.
  2. Start a new chat. Paste in your three-line spec from Exercise 1 instead.
  3. Compare the two answers. Which is closer to what you wanted? Write down two or three things you noticed.
Hand-in
  • Your need sentence and four jobs
  • One three-line spec with an acceptance test
  • Your never-break list
  • What your partner asked, and what you changed
  • Your notes from Exercise 2

Chapter recap

  • AI can write code; you decide what gets built, and you check it.
  • Start with the person and their need, not the features. The need isn't the first solution.
  • Break big ideas into small jobs you can build and test one at a time.
  • Write a three-line spec before asking an AI to build anything, and keep it up to date.
  • Name what can never break, and protect it in every decision.
  • Turn "how you'll know it works" into an acceptance test someone else can run.

Teacher box

Timing

About 2 lessons of 60-75 minutes. Lesson 1: the opener, the build loop, 1.1-1.2, and Exercise 1 steps 1-2. Lesson 2: 1.3-1.5, Exercise 1 steps 3-5 (including the spec swap), and Exercise 2.

Common sticking points

  • Need sentences that are really features ("an app with a leaderboard"). Ask: "What problem does the leaderboard solve, and for whom?"
  • Specs with no "doesn't do" line. Students resist limits; point back to the empty Celebrations board.
  • Untestable success lines ("it works well", "it looks good"). Ask: "What would you click, and what would you see?"
  • No AI access yet (approval pending, age rules). Exercise 1 is paper-only; Exercise 2 can be a teacher-run demo on the projector.

How to assess

  • The need sentence names a person, a situation and a need, with no feature in it.
  • Four jobs, each small enough to build and check on its own.
  • The spec has all three parts, and the acceptance test has a clear pass or fail.
  • The partner could explain the project back, or the student revised the spec after the swap.
  • Quick exit ticket: "Rewrite this solution as a need: 'a points system for my class.'"
