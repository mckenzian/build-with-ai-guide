Build, Test and Debug

You've planned (Chapter 1) and you've handed out jobs (Chapter 2). Now something gets built, and something goes wrong. That's not failure. That's the normal middle of every project. This chapter is about getting through it calmly.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Test like a troublemaker: twice, empty, weird, out of order.
  • Catch quiet bugs by asking "what happens the second time?"
  • Read an error message for what, where and when.
  • Fix a problem with one small change at a time, not a rewrite.
  • Keep saved copies so you can always go back.

A real moment: points paid twice

From the ALC live-class build

Students in my live-class tool already answered a question that ranked teams. I asked to turn it into a race, "Kahoot-style but much better," with points for each group. Before anything was built, the task note listed the open questions: does the race reward speed or accuracy, how do points add to group totals, does every teammate still get a turn, and what does the teacher's screen show?

I picked answers, and the AI built the game in four parts: the rules behind the scenes, the race lanes on the projector, the phone screens, and the final reveal with points and a podium. Then the reviewer bot said NO-GO on the reveal.

  1. Ideaa team race
  2. Specfour open questions answered
  3. AI buildsfour parts
  4. Reviewer: NO-GOdouble-click pays twice; ending early skips payout
  5. Controlled fixpay once per student, plus a new test

If the teacher double-clicked "Next," or two screens pressed it at once, a group could get its points twice. And if the class ended early, the payout could be skipped. The fix: points are recorded once per student and can never be paid a second time, and ending the class pays anything still owed. A new test hammers the button from many screens at once to prove it.

Key idea

Look at the shape: idea, spec, AI builds, test finds a problem, controlled fix, new test so it never comes back. Turning a bug into a test like this is called a regression test: it keeps an old bug from sneaking back (A. Perry & Luebbe, 2016). That's the whole chapter in one example.

What you need

  • A free AI chat assistant, ideally one that can show a working preview of a simple web page.
  • A free Google Doc for your test log and saved copies.

3.1Test like a troublemaker

The AI will usually test the "happy path": a user who clicks everything once, in order, like a perfect robot. Real people don't do that. They double-click. They press Back. They leave things blank. They use two devices at once.

AI-written work can look right and still be wrong. In a Stanford study, people who used an AI coding helper wrote less secure code than people who didn't, and were more confident that their code was secure (N. Perry et al., 2023). So you need your own checks.

Try to break it

  • Click things twice, fast.
  • Leave a box empty.
  • Type something weird: a very long name, an emoji, a number where words go.
  • Do things out of order.
  • Stop halfway through.
  • Refresh the page, and do it all again.

The points-paid-twice bug only showed up because someone asked: what if the button gets pressed twice?

3.2Looks right but isn't

The scariest bugs don't crash anything. They just quietly do the wrong thing.

From the ALC live-class build

The AI wrote a step that fills in each class's schedule when the app starts. On its own, it worked fine. But the reviewer bot spotted a catch: the step ran every time the app restarted and rewrote the class end times. So if a teacher fixed a class time by hand, the next restart quietly put the old time back. No error. No warning.

  1. Teacher fixes a time by hand
  2. App restarts
  3. Old time is backno error, no warning
  4. Fix: only fill blankssaved times stay

Programmers have a word for a step that is safe to repeat: idempotent. Stripe, a payments company, builds this in so that a retried request isn't carried out twice (Stripe, n.d.).

Rule of thumb

Ask: "What happens the second time?" Restart it. Refresh it. Run it again. Then check that what you saved is still there.

3.3Reading an error without panicking

Sometimes you do get an error message. It might look like gibberish. You don't need to understand all of it. Look for three things:

Look forWhat it means
WhatWhat it says went wrong, in plain-ish words like "not found" or "missing." Where to look depends on the tool. In Python, for example, the most useful line is usually the last one (Python Software Foundation, n.d.).
WhereWhere it happened: a page name, a button, a line number.
WhenWhat you did just before it happened.

Then give the AI all three, plus your spec:

Good bug report"I clicked Submit with the name box empty. I got this error: [paste]. My spec says empty names should show 'Please type your name.' What's wrong?"

From the ALC live-class build: a real error, read in plain words

While checking a change, the reviewer bot typed an absurdly huge class number (more than 19 digits long) into one of the app's web addresses. The page crashed, and the log said: OverflowError in teacher_owns_class → game.get_class.

Read it from left to right. "OverflowError" means the number was too big to store. The arrow shows the path: the permission check passed the number to the class lookup, and the lookup broke. So the new "reject huge numbers" check guarded one door (saving) but not another (the list of options). The bot tried three more huge numbers to be sure, then said exactly where the same check was needed.

3.4Small experiments, not a rewrite

When something breaks, it's tempting to say "just rewrite the whole thing." Be careful. A rewrite can throw away fixes that were already working and bring brand-new bugs. One well-known software writer called rewriting from scratch the worst mistake a software company can make (Spolsky, 2000). Small changes are also easier to undo (Fowler, 2024).

Instead, isolate the problem:

  1. Find the smallest action that causes it. ("It only breaks when I double-click Next.")
  2. Change one thing.
  3. Test again, including your earlier tests.
  4. If it's fixed, write down why. If not, undo that change and try a different one.

Notice the team challenge fix didn't rebuild the game. It changed one rule: points get recorded once per student. That's a controlled fix. AI researcher Andrej Karpathy works the same way with AI: in small chunks, checking each one before moving on (Y Combinator, 2025).

3.5When you don't understand the code, and how to undo

You don't have to understand the code to manage it. Ask the AI to explain, in plain words:

  • "Explain what this part does like I'm a new teammate."
  • "What would happen if someone clicked this twice?"
  • "What did you change since the last version, and why?"

If the explanation doesn't match your spec, that's a bug, even if nothing crashes.

Keep saved copies

Sometimes an AI change just makes things worse. That's why you keep saved copies of every version that worked. If the new one is broken, you go back.

You already know a tool that does this. In Google Docs, version history (open it from the "Last edit" button at the top right; you need edit access) lets you see and restore older versions (Google, n.d.). For your projects, do the same by hand: every time something works, paste it into your Doc under a heading like "Version 3: works, tested."

  1. Version 1works, tested
  2. Try a change
  3. Worse?go back to Version 1
  4. Version 2only if it passes

Programmers use a tool for this kind of undo history called version control; the most common one is called Git (Fowler, 2024). The idea is the same as your saved copies: a record of changes, so you can go back to an earlier version later (Chacon & Straub, 2014).

From the ALC live-class build: the change that would have undone someone else's work

I couldn't find a single time we had to roll back a change in the live-class tool; when something went wrong, the fix was a new, small change. The closest call came from one AI helper that built its change on an old copy of the project. If it had gone in, it would have quietly undone a teammate's newer work on counting participation. The reviewer bot compared it with the latest version and marked it NO-GO, and the change was rebuilt on top of the latest copy instead.

Coming up in Book 2

Your saved copies are the start of something bigger. In Book 2 we'll grow that habit into version control: a clear history of what changed, when and why. You'll learn to save a change (a commit), share it (push), and combine work from different people (merge) without overwriting each other. Then we'll add automatic checks that run when you share a change, so a test can catch a problem before people use the new version. One popular tool for this is GitHub Actions (GitHub, n.d.).

What you must check yourself

  • Did you try to break it? At least five troublemaker tests, including the second time.
  • Did you re-run your old tests after the fix? A fix can break something that used to work.
  • Do you have a saved copy of the last version that worked? Before every change.

Teacher box

Timing

2-3 lessons of 60-75 minutes. Lesson 1: opener, 3.1-3.2, start the exercise. Lesson 2: 3.3-3.5 and the fix cycle. Optional lesson 3: swap projects and test a partner's tool.

Common sticking points

  • "I couldn't find any bugs." Have a partner test it; fresh eyes find more. Make sure they tried refreshing and double-clicking.
  • Students ask the AI to "fix everything," get a whole new version, and lose what worked. Insist on Version 1 being saved first.
  • Students skip re-running old tests after a fix. Make the test table a required hand-in.
  • Free preview features vary. If previews aren't available, students can paste the AI's page into a free online HTML viewer your school approves, or walk through the AI's described behaviour.

How to assess

  • The test table has at least 5 varied tests, including a "second time" test.
  • The bug report to the AI includes what, where and when.
  • The fix is small and was re-tested, old tests included.
  • Saved copies exist and are labelled.
