Chapter 6 · The capstone

Capstone: A Real Tool for a Real Person

This is where everything comes together. You'll build one small tool for one real person, using the whole build loop. When you're done, you'll have something that works, that you can show, and that someone else could look after.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Find a real person and capture their need in their words.
  • Agree what "done" means before you build.
  • Run the whole build loop and keep a build log.
  • Write a half-page handoff note someone else could use.
  • Give a 2-minute demo that shows it works and that you checked it.

A real moment: how the live-class tool started

From the ALC live-class build

The live-class tool didn't start as a big plan. It started with a plain description of what students could already do: type a class code, wait, pick a name, and see a live scoreboard. At that point, that "join a live game" screen was the whole student side.

When I brought in AI helpers, I didn't tell them to build the finished thing. I told them to stop and ask me four questions first, and to wait for a yes:

  1. Which pages are needed?
  2. How do students sign in?
  3. How does it fit with the live game?
  4. Where do the lessons come from?

The same day, the first real job list was written:

  • Press "Run Live Class" and get a fresh class code.
  • Open attendance.
  • Allow only one live class per teacher at a time.
  • Clear students' mood check-ins at the start and end.
Key idea

Small, clear, testable. Everything else in this guide grew from there, and your capstone starts the same way. Making the AI ask before it builds is advice Ethan Mollick gives too: invite it to ask about anything that's unclear (Mollick, 2024).

6.1Pick a real person and a real need

Find someone who actually has a small problem: a teacher, a coach, a club leader, a family member, a classmate. Ask them about it. Listen for the need, not their first idea for a solution (remember Chapter 1). Professional teams do the same: they write the need from the user's point of view, then talk it through with the people they're building for (Rehkopf, n.d.).

Good sizeToo big
A sign-up form with a sheet that sorts itself, for a club leader"An app for the whole school"
A flashcard or quiz page for a classmate's test"A study app for every subject"
A practice-minutes tracker for a coach"A full team-management system"

If it can't be shown in 2 minutes, shrink it.

6.2What "done" means

Software teams agree on a "definition of done" up front, so everyone knows what finished means (Atlassian, n.d.). Your capstone is done when you have all six of these:

  1. The need: one sentence: person + situation + need.
  2. The spec: what it does, what it doesn't do, how you'll know it works.
  3. A passing acceptance test: the "how you'll know it works" line, actually tried, with the result written down. It must pass.
  4. A never-break list: the one to three things it must always get right, including keeping people's information safe.
  5. A 2-minute demo: you show it working, live, to the class or your person.
  6. A half-page handoff note (see 6.4).

6.3Work the loop

Use each chapter as a step:

ChapterWhat you do in the capstone
1 · PlanNeed, small jobs, spec, never-break list.
2 · DelegateGive the AI one job at a time with context; make it ask questions first; use a separate reviewer chat.
3 · Test and fixTest like a troublemaker; ask "what happens the second time?"; keep saved copies; make small fixes.
4 · Ship checkRun the ready-for-users checklist; use stand-ins, not real personal information; narrow share settings.
5 · Automate?Only if one part is truly repeated, and a human still approves what matters. It's fine to skip this.

Keep a short build log in a Google Doc as you go. For each feature, note:

  1. Idea
  2. Spec
  3. What the AI did
  4. Testand the problem it found
  5. Why the next change

That's exactly how the stories in this guide were told.

6.4The handoff note

In my experience, real tools often need looking after by someone other than the person who built them. Good notes are short, accurate, and kept up to date (Google, n.d.). Your handoff note is a half page with three parts:

PartWhat goes in it
How it worksIn plain words, what happens when someone uses it. Where the information lives.
What it can't doIts limits. What you chose to leave out. What could go wrong.
How to fix itWhere the saved copies are, what to check first if it breaks, and who to ask.
From the ALC live-class build: a real check report, as a model

After one update went live, the reviewer bot wrote this note (simplified):

  • Verdict: LIVE PASS.
  • What went out: the fix that closes live connections properly instead of leaving them open.
  • Server: started once, 0 restarts. Health check OK. All files match the approved version.
  • Since the update: 0 errors, 0 timeouts, 0 new issues in the error tracker.
  • How I checked: read-only. No sign-ins, no changes.
  • Blocking problems: none.

It's a check report rather than a full handoff note, but it shows the habits to copy: the verdict first, one line per check, how the checking was done, and what's left to do.

6.5The demo

You have 2 minutes. A good demo:

  1. The need15 seconds
  2. Main job, live60 seconds
  3. Test passing20 seconds
  4. One problem and fix25 seconds

Don't show everything. Show that it works and that you checked it.

What you must check yourself

  • Did your real person agree it meets their need? Ask them, don't assume.
  • Does your acceptance test pass today? Run it again before the demo.
  • Is there any real personal information in the tool, the test data or the AI chats?

Try it: your capstone

  1. Interview your real person. Write the need sentence.
  2. Write your spec and never-break list. Show your person: "Is this what you need?" Hand it to a classmate: "Can you tell what I'm building?"
  3. Build it with a free AI assistant and free tools (Google Forms, Sheets, Docs, or a simple page the AI makes). One job at a time.
  4. Test it. Record your acceptance test result and at least five troublemaker tests.
  5. Run the ready-for-users checklist.
  6. Write your half-page handoff note.
  7. Give your 2-minute demo.
  8. Write a short reflection: what went wrong, what you learned, what you'd do next.

Rubric

Four areas, each scored 1 to 4. The demo and handoff note are required to count as done; they are assessed as evidence in the Checking and Reflection rows.

LevelPlanChecking
1 · BeginningIdea with no clear person or need.Little or no testing.
2 · DevelopingNeed stated, but the spec is vague or missing a part.Acceptance test written but not passed or not recorded.
3 · ProficientClear need, three-part spec, never-break list.Acceptance test passes; several troublemaker tests recorded.
4 · ExcellentPlan was checked with the real person and a classmate, and changed based on what they said.Found a real problem, fixed it with a small change, re-ran tests, and kept saved copies.
LevelSafetyReflection
1 · BeginningUses real personal information or shares too widely.Describes what was built only.
2 · DevelopingSome safety steps, but gaps remain.Names a problem but not what was learned.
3 · ProficientStand-ins used; share settings narrow; checklist done.Explains what went wrong, why, and what changed.
4 · ExcellentCollects only what's needed; run-twice test done; safety choices explained in the handoff note.Connects decisions to the build loop and says clearly what they'd do next and why.

Chapter recap

  • Build one small tool for one real person.
  • Done = need, spec, passing acceptance test, never-break list, 2-minute demo, half-page handoff note.
  • Work the whole loop and keep a build log.
  • AI can write much of the tool. You decided what was worth building, and you checked it.

Teacher box

Timing

4-6 lessons of 60-75 minutes. Lesson 1: pick a person, interview, need and spec. Lessons 2-4: build, test, fix (check build logs each lesson). Lesson 5: ready-for-users check and handoff note. Lesson 6: demos (about 15 demos per lesson at 2 minutes plus changeover).

Common sticking points

  • Projects too big. Approve each spec before building starts; the 2-minute demo rule is a good size check.
  • No real person. Let students use you, another teacher or a classmate as the "client."
  • Free-tier message limits stall a build. A saved spec and build log in a Google Doc let students restart quickly in a new chat.
  • Real personal information creeps in. Check test data at the ship-check lesson.
  • Handoff notes turn into a list of features. Point back to the three headings: how it works, what it can't do, how to fix it.

How to assess

  • Use the rubric (Plan, Checking, Safety, Reflection).
  • Confirm all six "done" items exist before scoring.
  • The build log is the best evidence of real checking; weight it over polish.
  • A simple tool that's honestly tested should score higher than a flashy one that wasn't checked.
